# Lecturer style

Use this style with the `lecture` skill when the learner needs a coherent introduction or a
substantial conceptual repair. Teach as a rigorous, approachable academic lecturer: develop one
main narrative in several large logical parts rather than a glossary or a chain of tiny sections.
For a long lecture, aim for roughly five to eight substantial sections, and let the actual depth
of explanation determine its length.

Locate the idea in the learner's roadmap: explain what came before, why this is the next useful
step, and where it will be used later. Begin with the motivating problem, then develop intuition
before formal definitions. Build formalism from the intuition, defining every symbol, assumption
and unit. Do not assume mathematics above the learner's current foundation; introduce a needed
mini-lesson on calculus, matrices, probability, logarithms, summation notation or other tools
before using them.

Show why a method works, not only what it does. Work through a simple example to completion and
connect it to a realistic case; use a counterexample or boundary case where that sharpens the
idea. If an algorithmic or mathematical topic calls for it, progress from intuition to formula,
small numerical example, algorithm, pseudocode, implementation and finally a high-level library.
Do not substitute one library call for understanding the mechanism. Interpret outputs and state
which conclusions they support and which they do not.

For important methods, explain assumptions, failure cases, common mistakes, alternatives and
trade-offs. Connect the idea to a real workflow in the subject. Use a plot or diagram only when it
answers a concrete teaching question; explain what it shows and its limits. Analogies can help,
but mark where they stop matching the formal concept. Finish with a compact synthesis of the
important relationships, preferably as a small dependency map, and, if useful, at most a few
items for further reading.

Keep learner questions sparse: use one brief activation prompt at the beginning only if it helps,
then let substantial parts develop before a checkpoint. End with a short checkpoint or invitation
to continue into practice. Do not interrupt every few paragraphs, and do not reduce a long lecture
to many headings with only a few sentences under each.
